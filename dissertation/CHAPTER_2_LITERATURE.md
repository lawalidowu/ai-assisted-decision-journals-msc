# Chapter 2: Literature review

This chapter reviews literature on generating candidate decision records from public inquiry text at feasible scale, and on deciding which candidates should enter an auditable decision journal. Sections 2.1–2.2 cover decision journaling, crisis governance and LLM-based extraction. Sections 2.3–2.4 examine provenance, traceability and human evaluation, including the distinction between evidence strength and journal validity. Sections 2.5–2.6 situate the UK COVID-19 Inquiry case study and state the research gap.

## 2.1 Decision journals and crisis governance

Structured recording of decisions and supporting rationale has been advocated as useful practice in organisational learning and audit [2], [3]. Accountability literature emphasises that records should capture not only what was decided but the evidence and reasoning behind it, and that office-holders remain answerable for how decisions are documented [2]. In crisis governance, the same records support after-action review [1]. Public inquiries extend this logic to society-wide events: they provide an institutional mechanism for retrospective scrutiny of major incidents [8], as reflected in the UK COVID-19 Inquiry’s Terms of Reference, which require examination of how core decisions were made and what lessons should be learned [7].

As defined in Chapter 1 (Section 1.1), a decision journal is a structured record that pairs a decision claim with supporting evidence, provenance and contextual information needed for later review. Hearing transcripts, however, are not decision journals. They are performative, adversarial and temporally layered — witnesses recall past COBR meetings, counsel probes inconsistencies, and procedural business interrupts substantive testimony. Transforming transcripts into journal entries therefore requires an operational definition of what counts as a decision (agreements, adopted measures, authoritative directions) and a provenance discipline (every claim anchored to a verbatim quote). The transformation is treated here as an information-extraction and accountability problem rather than a deployment exercise for inquiry staff.

In inquiry dialogue, ministerial urging, retrospective recommendations and narrative descriptions can resemble decisions in surface form while failing the operational journal definition. The risks of over-generation documented in foundation-model research make this distinction important [5], [6]. Extraction therefore produces candidates; acceptance into a decision journal requires additional validation (Sections 2.3–2.4).

Decision journals can also be reviewed through recognised policy frameworks once the underlying evidence has been validated. In public-health emergency review, the Joint External Evaluation provides categories for preparedness capacities [9], while Decision Quality focuses on features of the decision process, including framing, information, values, alternatives, reasoning and follow-through [10], [11]. These frameworks support structured interpretation; they do not replace source traceability or the judgement of whether an extracted item belongs in the journal.

## 2.2 NLP and information extraction from long documents

Information extraction (IE) from long documents traditionally combined linguistic preprocessing, pattern rules and supervised models [12], [13]. Transformer-based pre-training (for example BERT) improved contextual representation for span extraction and classification [14], but many encoder pipelines still have bounded input lengths that make full long-document processing difficult [5], [15]. Modern LLMs may support longer context windows, yet longer context does not itself resolve provenance, discourse heterogeneity or artefact-validity problems. Inquiry transcripts also mix genres (question, answer, quotation, procedural). Task-specific supervised pipelines are costly to adapt when corpora, schemas or accountability requirements change.

Large language models shift the IE paradigm toward prompt-based extraction: a schema and instructions in natural language, few-shot or zero-shot, without task-specific fine-tuning [4]. Foundation-model surveys document both opportunity (flexible schemas, rapid prototyping) and risk (hallucination, inconsistent formatting, sensitivity to prompt wording) [5], [6]. Brown et al. support in-context task adaptation; they do not establish reliable decision-journal extraction, provenance preservation or public-sector auditability on their own.

Chunking long transcripts into overlapping sentence windows is adopted here as a reproducible and auditable processing unit: each window is processed independently with a structured schema (for example decision text, evidence, verbatim quote, source location). Chunking sacrifices global discourse coherence for auditability — each item’s quote must resolve within its window’s processed text — rather than because no contemporary model could admit longer inputs. Alternative architectures such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) [16] offer different provenance trade-offs. At this pilot’s bounded scale, complete fixed chunks were supplied directly to the model, which simplified item-level provenance inspection; a retrieval index was therefore not required for the chosen design.

Most IE literature evaluates whether models extract the right spans or labels, not whether extracted items should enter a target artefact. Prompt-based extraction addresses scalability, but accountability-oriented use requires treating the extractor as stage one of a multi-stage pipeline; traceability checks and post-extraction validation follow in Sections 2.3–2.4.

## 2.3 Grounding, traceability, and provenance

Accountable use of AI in government contexts emphasises transparency, traceability and human oversight [17], [18], [19]. For extraction pipelines, grounding means that every structured claim can be verified against source text [20], [21].

Mechanical traceability checks — verifying that each quoted span is locatable in the processed source after normalisation — implement grounding in auditable form. Traceability pass is necessary but not sufficient: human–machine agreement on content can co-occur with traceability failure (for example long quotes broken by PDF layout), and conversely a locatable quote may support the wrong kind of statement (procedural adjournment versus policy decision).

### 2.3.1 Traceability is not journal membership

Traceability establishes source provenance — that a quoted anchor exists in the source record. It does not establish journal membership — that the extracted statement belongs in a policy decision journal. A hearing adjournment can be quoted faithfully and pass traceability while failing journal validity; conversely, a valid policy decision may fail traceability because of PDF layout artefacts. Stratified human validation can show how often faithful extraction of non-journal discourse occurs within the evaluated sample.

Structural consistency and traceability therefore form the auditable floor; journal validity and evidence strength require separate human assessment (Section 2.4). LLM self-reported confidence is better treated as a hypothesis for empirical comparison against human rubrics than as ground truth in high-stakes settings [5], [6].

## 2.4 Human evaluation of LLM outputs

Qualitative technology evaluation requires human judgement at bounded sample depth plus corpus-scale automated metrics at breadth [22]. Towler et al. [23] propose MATA triangulation for comparing human and machine analysis of COVID-19 policy text: convergence categories of agreement, silence (present in one method only) and dissonance (genuine disagreement). MATA supports purposive comparison of human and machine extractions on the same text regions without requiring full-corpus manual coding.

Bondaronek et al. [24] introduce GRACE, a framework for evaluating AI-generated textual outputs on dimensions including interpretability, actionability, nuance and redundancy. As an unpublished preprint at the time of writing, GRACE has not undergone formal peer review. Its use here is treated only as a supplementary descriptive quality lens on a bounded sample, not as a validated benchmark for decision-journal correctness. Broader LLM evaluation research similarly treats human judgement and multi-criteria rubrics as central to assessing generated text [25].

### 2.4.1 Validity versus evidence strength

Neither MATA nor GRACE directly answers the question: should this extracted item be accepted into the decision journal? MATA compares human and machine identification of themes or decisions; GRACE rates quality dimensions of generated text. Both are useful, but each addresses only part of the problem. Neither separates journal validity — whether an entry belongs in the target artefact — from evidence strength — how strongly the quoted evidence supports the extracted decision text. That distinction is familiar from faithfulness evaluation in abstractive summarisation [26] and from broader debates on what human evaluation of NLP systems should measure [22]. Conflating the two dimensions — for example, scoring a procedural adjournment as “low confidence” because it is not a policy decision — would confuse two different questions: whether the extraction is well supported by the source and whether the extracted item belongs in the journal.

This gap motivates dual-rubric human validation: one rubric for journal validity and one for quote-to-decision support. Towler’s human-in-the-loop framing [23] supports structured annotation on bounded samples. Because the two rubrics assess different questions, later review aids also serve different purposes. Flags mark entries requiring caution without removing them; confidence comparisons test automated signals against evidence-strength ratings; and clustering helps reviewers navigate related candidates. None replaces human judgement. Together, they provide information that a single evaluation score cannot capture. Automated semantic-similarity metrics were not adopted as primary endpoints here; traceability, MATA-style triangulation and rubric-based human assessment align better with high-stakes extraction tasks than semantic similarity alone.

Collectively, these studies suggest that no single evaluation framework adequately captures both extraction correctness and decision usefulness in high-stakes policy text. This motivates the multi-layer evaluation adopted in the present study, where structural consistency, traceability, evidence strength and journal validity are assessed separately. Post-extraction enrichment then operates on a fixed reference dataset of candidate decision-journal entries without altering the underlying extraction results.

## 2.5 UK COVID-19 Inquiry as case study

The UK COVID-19 Inquiry public document archive provides reproducible primary data: hearing PDFs, metadata and supplementary reports [27]. Module 2 focuses on core UK decision-making and political governance — COBR, COVID-O, ministerial evidence and cross-government coordination. In this dissertation, Module 2 is treated as an empirical case study of a broader AI-assisted decision-journaling problem for complex public records, not as the sole intended application of the method.

The pilot corpus comprises eight hearing transcripts (28 November 2023 – 23 May 2024), selected for complete PDF availability, mix of witness types and coverage of early and late Module 2 sessions. Three hearing days received deeper manual review; five additional days support corpus-scale metrics. A report-genre pilot on one Module 2 “In Brief” PDF illustrates extraction on structured narrative prose as a supplementary genre contrast.

The case study combines realistic policy and institutional vocabulary, public reproducibility and inherent difficulty (recalled decisions, advocacy, procedural content). Public inquiry hearings are adversarial, performative records rather than neutral administrative documents [8], which limits generalisation beyond this genre.

## 2.6 Summary and research gap

Existing research suggests that large language models can adapt to extraction-like tasks through in-context prompting [4]. That capability does not by itself establish accountable decision-journal records. Reliability for accountability-oriented extraction still depends on explicit provenance and validation.

Parallel strands show that provenance and traceability improve trustworthiness in accountable AI use [17], [18], [19], and that human evaluation remains necessary in high-stakes domains [22], [23], [24]. Traceability establishes source linkage, but it does not establish whether an extracted item belongs in a decision journal. Questions such as journal validity and evidence strength therefore still require human assessment, and generated candidate statements must remain distinguishable from validated source evidence.

These strands are commonly treated separately. Extraction studies focus on span or schema correctness; grounding work focuses on source linkage; evaluation frameworks focus on output quality or human–machine convergence. Few studies examine how extracted outputs should be governed before they are incorporated into an auditable decision record.

The remaining gap is therefore practical as well as technical. Public inquiry text mixes genuine decisions with procedure, counsel questions, advocacy and retrospective narrative. The literature offers limited guidance on combining source-linked candidate extraction, bounded human review, separate assessment of journal validity and evidence strength, and supplementary aids for reviewer navigation and structured policy interpretation within one governed workflow. Retrieval-augmented generation addresses access to external knowledge [16], but it does not by itself determine whether an extracted item belongs in the target decision journal.

The present work responds through layered extraction and post-extraction evaluation and review at pilot scale, rather than by proposing a new extraction algorithm. The extractor is treated as a candidate generator; validation and review layers assemble an auditable decision-journal prototype for human inspection. Post-extraction enrichment operates on a fixed candidate dataset without altering the original extraction results. Clustering and framework interpretation support navigation and structured reading of validated source passages; they do not establish extraction correctness on their own.

The contribution is therefore a governed validation and review process for assessing extracted candidates within an auditable decision-journal workflow. It is not a deployment-ready system or a new extraction algorithm.
