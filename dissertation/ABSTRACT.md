# Abstract

Important decisions made during public emergencies are often difficult to reconstruct because the supporting evidence is dispersed across complex public records. Public inquiry transcripts illustrate the problem: substantive decisions appear alongside questions, procedure, advocacy and retrospective narrative. This dissertation examines AI-assisted decision journaling as a method for creating and reviewing traceable candidate entries from such records, using UK COVID-19 Inquiry Module 2 hearing transcripts as a case study.

Eight hearing transcripts produced 414 candidate entries, each checked to confirm that its quoted passage could be located in the processed transcript. The candidates were stored unchanged for later review and flagged where caution was needed, without deleting entries. In a stratified sample of 50 entries (n = 50), selected across different candidate groups, the entries were assessed on two separate questions: whether each item belonged in the decision journal and how strongly its quotation supported the extracted statement; automated confidence was compared with those judgements. Exploratory clustering grouped all 414 candidates for navigation. A purposive sample of 60 entries (n = 60) tested Joint External Evaluation and Decision Quality mapping and whether generated summaries accurately reflected their source passages, using human review of the transcript evidence.

Of the 414 candidates, 351 passed the source-location check. In the n = 50 sample, the most frequent pattern was 21/50 items where the quotation strongly supported the generated statement but the item did not meet the decision-journal definition. Automated confidence tracked quote support more closely than judgements about journal inclusion. In the n = 60 pilot, an observable Decision Quality element was identified more often than a defensible Joint External Evaluation mapping (37/60 versus 11/60), while generated wording did not always reflect the source closely enough to be treated as evidence on its own.

The findings support keeping candidate generation separate from later evaluation and treating validated source passages as authoritative. The main contribution is a governed extraction-and-review process that may support source-linked retrospective review and organisational learning. The conclusions remain bounded by the eight-transcript case study and single-reviewer assessments; stronger independent human validation should precede wider scaling.
